How Much Mortgage Can You Afford?

Our affordability calculator helps you determine your maximum home purchase price based on Canadian mortgage qualification rules. We calculate both your Gross Debt Service (GDS) and Total Debt Service (TDS) ratios to ensure you meet lender requirements. That is the same framework a TD mortgage affordability calculator uses: income, existing debts, down payment, and a qualifying rate—not just the contract rate on a flyer.

What You'll Need:

  • Annual Income: Your gross household income before taxes
  • Monthly Debts: Car payments, credit cards, student loans, etc.
  • Down Payment: How much you have saved for a down payment
  • Property Expenses: Estimated property taxes, heating, and condo fees

Understanding GDS and TDS Ratios

Canadian lenders use two key ratios to determine how much you can borrow:

GDS Ratio (Gross Debt Service)

Maximum 39% of your gross monthly income can go toward housing costs.

Includes: Mortgage payment + Property taxes + Heating + 50% of condo fees

TDS Ratio (Total Debt Service)

Maximum 44% of your gross monthly income can go toward all debts.

Includes: Housing costs + Car payments + Credit cards + Student loans + Other debts

The Mortgage Stress Test

Since 2018, Canadian homebuyers must qualify at a higher interest rate than their actual contract rate. This "stress test" ensures you can afford your mortgage even if rates rise.

Current Stress Test Rate:

The higher of: 5.25% OR your contract rate + 2.00%

If a lender offers a 4.50% contract and the stress test applies, the qualifying rate is 6.50% because 4.50 plus 2 is above the 5.25% floor. Type 6.50 into this calculator. It does not add the 2 points on its own.

How this affordability calculator sizes a mortgage

The tool works backwards from income. It takes gross annual income, divides by 12, and allows housing costs up to 39% of that month and housing plus other debts up to 44%. Those two ceilings are the gross debt service and total debt service limits CMHC publishes for insured mortgages. OSFI does not publish 39% and 44% as a universal cap for every uninsured file, so a bank can be stricter. The tighter of the two ratios sets the maximum payment. Property tax, heating, and half of the condo fee you type are subtracted before any payment is left for the mortgage. Half of the condo fee matches the usual insurer worksheet. It is not 100%.

The interest rate in the form is the qualifying rate the payment is calculated at. The default is 5.25%. The tool does not look up a contract rate and add 2 percentage points for you. If a lender has offered 4% and the stress test applies, type the greater of 6% and 5.25% into the field yourself. A same-lender renewal, or a straight switch that does not add money or years, may not use that qualifying rate at all. This page is for a new purchase or a refinance, where the test usually applies.

The payment formula is the same monthly compounding the mortgage calculator uses: the annual qualifying rate divided by 12. A lender’s disclosure on a fixed rate usually compounds semi-annually, so the signed payment can differ by a few dollars. That gap is small next to the difference between the contract payment and the qualifying payment. The mortgage stress test guide explains the qualifying-rate rule. The stress test qualifier runs one mortgage amount instead of solving for the maximum.

Down payment, insurance, and the price cap this tool does not apply

If the loan is more than 80% of the price the tool just solved for, it adds a default-insurance premium to the mortgage and recalculates the payment. The bands inside this affordability tool are simple: down payment under 10% uses 4.00% of the loan, 10% to under 15% uses 3.10%, and 15% to 20% uses 2.80%. At 20% down or more the premium is zero. Those three bands are a shortcut. They are not the full CMHC schedule, and they do not add the extra 0.20 point CMHC charges when the amortization is longer than 25 years.

This affordability tool also does not enforce the insured price cap. A purchase price of $1,500,000 or more cannot be insured, and the minimum down payment becomes 20%. The CMHC insurance calculator applies that cap and the traditional premium table. Use it once you have a real price. Use this page only to see how far income and the qualifying rate stretch.

First-time buyer programs change the cash you need, not the ratio math. The FHSA, the Home Buyers’ Plan, and provincial land-transfer refunds are described in the first-time home buyer programs guide. A larger down payment can push the loan to 80% or below and remove the premium. It does not remove the stress test on a new insured or uninsured purchase from a federally regulated lender. Confirm the payment with the lender before you waive a financing condition.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does this calculator include the stress test?

It uses the qualifying rate you type. The field defaults to 5.25% and does not automatically add 2 percentage points to a contract rate. If the stress test applies, enter the greater of the contract rate plus 2 points and 5.25% yourself. Condo fees are counted at 50%.

Can I use this as a TD mortgage affordability calculator?

Yes for a planning estimate. TD and other Big 5 banks use GDS, TDS, and a qualifying-rate stress test to size a mortgage. Enter a TD contract rate from our TD lender page to approximate how much house TD might allow, then confirm with a TD specialist. Lender overlays, property type, and down-payment rules can still change the result.

What if my down payment is less than 20%?

If your down payment is less than 20%, you'll need mortgage default insurance (CMHC/Sagen/Canada Guaranty). The calculator includes this premium in your total mortgage amount.

Can I afford more if I have a co-signer?

Yes, adding a co-signer with income can increase your affordability. Include their annual income in the calculator, but remember their debts will also be included in the TDS calculation.
